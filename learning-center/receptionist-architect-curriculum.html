01 · Call triage

Recognize intent, urgency and scope

A good receptionist first identifies why the person is calling. It separates urgent service requests, routine estimates, existing-customer questions, appointment requests and matters that require a human. It should follow the business's written emergency criteria—not invent its own.

  • Define call types and their required next actions.
  • Write explicit escalation triggers, after-hours rules and fallback contacts.
  • Collect location and callback details when needed for the approved response.
  • Never promise dispatch, arrival, diagnosis or resolution unless a trusted system confirms it.

Example: A caller reports active water entering a property. The agent acknowledges urgency, gathers the minimum details required by the approved procedure and routes the call to the designated emergency path. It does not guarantee a technician's arrival.

02 · Knowledge quality

Use approved information—and admit uncertainty

AI receptionists need a defined source of truth. Business hours, service areas, pricing rules, warranties and appointment availability can change. A fluent answer is not necessarily a correct answer.

  • Identify the authoritative owner and update process for each fact.
  • Set precedence when website copy, internal policy and calendar data conflict.
  • Separate fixed facts from live data that must be checked at call time.
  • When information is missing or contradictory, say so and offer a callback or human handoff.

Example: If a caller asks for a warranty duration absent from approved materials, the agent should not infer an industry-standard number. It should capture the question and route it for confirmation.

03 · Lead intake

Capture useful details without over-collecting

Lead qualification is not an interrogation. Ask for information that supports a legitimate next step and is proportionate to the service request.

  • Typical service intake: name, callback number, service address or area, job type and a concise description.
  • Ask one question at a time and confirm speech-recognition uncertainties.
  • Distinguish urgent work, active projects and future opportunities.
  • Do not request passwords, payment credentials or unrelated sensitive information.
  • Define how duplicates, incomplete records and callback requests are handled.

Example: A roof-replacement prospect planning for next year should be tagged as a future opportunity, not an emergency. Capture their timeline and preferred follow-up.

04 · Scheduling integrity

Never turn a suggestion into a confirmed booking

Scheduling requires reliable access to the right calendar, correct time-zone handling and a clear distinction between checking availability, holding a slot and completing a booking.

  • Use the business's authoritative scheduling source.
  • Refresh stale availability before presenting it as current.
  • Repeat date, time and time zone; confirm the booking only after tool success.
  • Define what happens when a calendar call times out, partially succeeds or conflicts.
  • Never invent a slot to satisfy a caller.

Example: If the calendar booking succeeds but the CRM write fails, communicate only the confirmed booking and trigger the documented retry or human follow-up for the failed CRM update.

05 · Human handoff

Make escalation a designed feature

Automation should know its boundaries. Human handoff is essential for exceptions, complaints, complex questions, sensitive requests and failed tools.

  • Define who receives each escalation and during which hours.
  • Provide a tested fallback when a transfer is unanswered or unavailable.
  • Capture the caller's callback details and reason for contact when appropriate.
  • Tell the caller what actually happened; never pretend a transfer succeeded.
  • Measure abandoned handoffs and unresolved callbacks.

Example: If a transfer tool fails, the agent explains that the connection did not complete, follows the backup notification path and confirms the next step without claiming a person answered.

06 · Privacy and consent

Collect, use and retain only what the workflow needs

Phone workflows may handle personal information, recordings, transcripts and messages. The right design depends on the business, jurisdiction, vendors, purpose and actual configuration. A general guide is not a legal determination.

  • Document what information is collected, why, where it goes and who can access it.
  • Use appropriate notice and consent procedures for recording, transcription and messaging.
  • Restrict access by role and business/customer; do not expose one customer's records to another.
  • Define retention, deletion, correction and privacy-request escalation paths.
  • Never claim that data was deleted or a request completed unless the authorized system confirms it.

Example: If a caller asks to erase records but the agent cannot verify identity or invoke an approved deletion workflow, it should explain the request path and escalate—not claim deletion.

Requirements vary by location, industry and call direction. Have qualified counsel review the actual call flows and disclosures before deployment.

07 · AI and tool security

Treat caller instructions and retrieved content as untrusted input

A caller, web page or document can contain instructions that conflict with the agent's authorized task. Tool access must be scoped independently of what the conversation asks the model to do.

  • Keep system instructions and business policy separate from caller-provided content.
  • Do not reveal hidden prompts, credentials or internal configuration.
  • Apply least privilege to CRM, calendar, messaging and administrative tools.
  • Require authenticated authorization and audit records for privileged changes.
  • Validate tool inputs and outputs; confirm the actual result before making a promise.

Example: A caller claims to be the owner and asks the receptionist to disable call screening. Without an authenticated administrative workflow, the agent must decline the configuration change and route the request appropriately.

08 · Conversation design

Make the interaction clear, calm and human-centered

Good call handling is not just a correct answer. The caller needs to understand the question, the next step and what the business can realistically do.

  • Ask concise questions and avoid collecting several details at once.
  • Acknowledge frustration without arguing, blaming or admitting unverified fault.
  • Support only languages and workflows that have been tested and configured.
  • Handle interruptions by addressing the immediate need, then resuming only essential questions.
  • Close by summarizing the action and setting accurate expectations.

Example: If a caller is upset about a missed callback, acknowledge the experience, capture the relevant details and follow the approved follow-up path without promising an unverified response time.

09 · Testing and quality

Measure accuracy and safety—not just conversion

A change that increases bookings but also increases false promises may make the business worse off. Quality needs explicit guardrails and repeatable testing.

  • Build test cases for normal calls, edge cases, interruptions and tool failures.
  • Review transcripts and outcomes with appropriate access controls and retention.
  • Track qualified lead capture, successful handoffs, booking accuracy, unresolved calls and complaints.
  • Test after every material prompt, integration or policy change.
  • Use a rollback path when a change causes harmful or unreliable behavior.

Example: If a prompt revision creates more bookings but invents appointment availability, pause or roll back the change, audit the failures and retest before release.

IMPLEMENTATION CHECKLIST

Before a receptionist answers a real call.

  • Written business facts, approved answers and a named owner for updates.
  • Call types, required intake fields, escalation triggers and after-hours rules.
  • Calendar/CRM permissions, failure handling and confirmation behavior.
  • Recording, transcription, SMS, privacy notice and retention decisions reviewed for the actual deployment.
  • Test scenarios for urgent calls, ambiguous questions, unsupported requests, tool failure and human handoff.
  • Monitoring, complaint review, change control and rollback responsibilities.

Use this checklist as a starting point. It does not replace industry-specific policies, security review, contracts or legal advice.
