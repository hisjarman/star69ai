AI Receptionist vs. Human Receptionist: Which Costs Your Business Less in 2026?

A full-time receptionist and an AI phone service do different jobs. Compare current U.S. wage data, service fees, call volume and the human work that still needs a person before choosing.

By Solomon Jarman · Founder & Operator Updated November 20, 2026 11 min read

When owners compare an AI receptionist vs. human receptionist, the lowest monthly price can look like the obvious winner. But the two options are not interchangeable. A person may answer the phone, greet visitors, coordinate a busy front desk, update records and handle unusual situations. An AI receptionist is usually purchased to cover a defined set of phone workflows.

For a business that mainly needs calls answered, routine questions handled and messages or lead details captured, an AI service may require a much smaller cash outlay than hiring another full-time employee. If you need someone physically at the front desk or making judgment calls across many administrative tasks, that is not a like-for-like replacement. The right comparison starts with the work—not the label.

Short answer
AI is often less expensive for clearly defined phone coverage, especially when calls arrive outside office hours or in short bursts. Human receptionists remain valuable for in-person service, complex judgment and work beyond the phone. Many small businesses should compare a hybrid model before choosing either extreme.

What does a human receptionist really cost?

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports a national median receptionist wage of $18.27 per hour, or $38,010 per year, in May 2025. That is a useful public benchmark, not a quote for your location or a complete employer-cost estimate. Pay varies by market, industry, experience, schedule and responsibilities.

Wages are only one part of the budget. Depending on the position and the benefits you provide, an employer may also need to account for:

  • Employer payroll taxes and unemployment contributions.
  • Health, retirement or other benefits offered to employees.
  • Paid time off, sick leave and holiday coverage.
  • Recruiting, background checks, onboarding and training.
  • Computer, phone, desk space, software and supervision.
  • Backup coverage when the receptionist is away or the phone queue is busy.

For context, the BLS reported that benefits represented 30.0% of average total compensation costs for private-industry workers in June 2026. That figure covers private-industry workers across occupations; it is not a receptionist-specific benefits rate and should not be applied mechanically to the receptionist wage above. It does show why comparing an AI subscription with an employee's hourly wage alone leaves out important costs. See the BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release for the underlying data.

Also consider what the person does when the phone is quiet. If the receptionist greets visitors, processes paperwork, coordinates deliveries or supports the office, those duties have value too. Do not charge the entire position to phone answering if phone work is only one part of the role.

What does an AI receptionist cost?

AI receptionist pricing is provider-specific. A useful quote should explain the complete monthly cost, not just a low per-minute headline. Ask whether the plan includes voice processing, phone service, number rental, transfers, usage alerts, setup, call-flow changes, support and any required integrations.

Common cost components include:

  • Subscription: the recurring plan fee and the features or support included.
  • Included minutes: how many AI-handled voice minutes are covered and how usage is measured.
  • Additional usage: the rate after included usage and any available rollover or Minute Vault balance is exhausted.
  • Setup: one-time configuration, call-flow design, business knowledge preparation and testing.
  • Telephony: phone numbers, forwarding, carrier charges or transfer costs that may be separate.
  • Optional work: custom integrations, specialized workflows or additional support that are outside the plan.

Ask what happens when a call is transferred but nobody answers, when a caller hangs up quickly, when the system retries, or when a call exceeds the included allowance. Confirm whether charges are automatic, whether you can set a hard limit and how the provider handles an outage or failed integration.

How to compare the costs fairly

Use the same call workload and service expectations for both options. A simple comparison can be built in three steps.

  1. Define the phone workload. Count inbound calls, average connected minutes, busy periods, after-hours demand, common questions, transfers and appointment requests. Separate phone tasks from in-person or office-administration work.
  2. Calculate the human cost for the work being compared. Start with local compensation, then add the employer costs that apply. If the receptionist also handles visitors and paperwork, allocate only the relevant share of the role's cost to phone coverage—or compare the full role with the full scope of work.
  3. Calculate the AI cost plus remaining human work. Add the subscription, setup, actual billable usage, phone charges and approved add-ons. Then include the staff time needed to review messages, handle escalations, correct business information and maintain the customer relationship.
Monthly comparison formula
Human phone-coverage cost = allocated wages + applicable employer costs + coverage and training costs.

AI phone-coverage cost = subscription + setup allocation + billable usage + telephony/add-ons + staff time for exceptions and follow-up.

Then compare the result with the same outcome measures: calls answered, qualified details captured, successful transfers, callback completion, caller complaints and staff time saved. Do not treat a call answered by AI as a booked appointment or a sale. Those are separate outcomes that need their own records.

When a human receptionist is the better investment

Choose a person—or keep a person central—when the role includes work that cannot be reduced to a phone script. Examples include:

  • Welcoming visitors, patients, tenants or clients in person.
  • Handling sensitive complaints or conversations that require discretion.
  • Making decisions that depend on context, policy exceptions or human judgment.
  • Coordinating several office tasks beyond answering calls.
  • Supporting callers who need a patient, flexible human conversation.

The BLS description of receptionist work includes answering and forwarding calls, scheduling appointments, greeting visitors, entering information and maintaining documents. That range matters: replacing the phone portion of the job does not automatically replace the entire position.

When an AI receptionist may be the more economical choice

An AI receptionist is worth evaluating when your needs are narrower and repeatable:

  • Answering routine questions using approved business information.
  • Capturing a new caller's name, contact details and reason for calling.
  • Providing a consistent next step when staff are busy or the office is closed.
  • Routing calls using clear, tested rules.
  • Handling predictable first-contact tasks so staff can focus on work that needs them.

It should not be treated as an unlimited substitute for human judgment. Speech recognition can be affected by background noise, unusual names, accents, interruptions and ambiguous requests. A transfer can fail. An integration can be unavailable. The service needs a safe response when it does not understand, and the business needs a person who owns the next step.

Why a hybrid model often makes sense

For many small businesses, the practical answer is not AI or human. It is AI for well-defined first contact and a person for the situations that need judgment. For example, an AI receptionist might collect the caller's service type, location and preferred callback time, then route an urgent request according to an approved on-call procedure. A staff member can review the details and decide what happens next.

Before launch, test normal calls, interruptions, noisy audio, unknown questions, after-hours requests, failed transfers and requests for a person. Make sure the greeting is transparent about the automated service, the escalation destination is staffed and the system never promises a booking, dispatch or callback that has not actually been confirmed.

Seven questions to ask before you decide

  1. What work must be covered? List phone duties separately from visitor reception, paperwork and office administration.
  2. What is the complete monthly cost? Include wages and applicable employer costs for a person, or subscription, usage, telephony and add-ons for AI.
  3. What happens outside normal hours? Confirm the schedule, routing, on-call rules and what callers hear when nobody is available.
  4. Who configures and maintains the AI? Ask whether setup, testing, knowledge updates and ongoing call-flow changes are included or billed separately.
  5. How are exceptions handled? Test a failed transfer, unknown answer, complaint, urgent request and caller who asks for a person.
  6. What data is collected? Review recordings, transcripts, access controls, retention, deletion, consent and any third-party processing before enabling the service.
  7. How will you judge success? Agree on answer rate, successful handoffs, message quality, callback completion, caller feedback and total cost per handled call.

Frequently asked questions

Is an AI receptionist cheaper than hiring a receptionist in 2026?

It can be less expensive for defined phone coverage, especially when compared with adding a full-time position solely to answer calls. But the AI plan does not automatically replace in-person reception, administrative work or human judgment. Compare total costs and equivalent responsibilities.

Does the BLS receptionist wage include benefits?

No. The BLS May 2025 figure is a wage statistic. Employer benefits, payroll-related costs, paid leave and other expenses are separate considerations. The BLS June 2026 employer-cost data provide broad private-industry context, not a receptionist-specific benefit estimate.

Can an AI receptionist replace a part-time employee?

Possibly for a narrow phone workflow, but first identify what the employee does beyond answering calls. If the role includes front-desk or administrative duties, those responsibilities still need an owner.

Will AI answer every call or book every appointment?

No service should promise that. Provider availability, call routing, caller behavior, integrations and configuration can all affect the result. Ask for a realistic test and a clear fallback process.

Do overtime rules affect receptionist cost?

For employees covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act who are nonexempt, overtime is generally due after 40 hours in a workweek at not less than one-and-one-half times the regular rate. State rules and individual circumstances can differ, so confirm the applicable requirements with a qualified payroll or employment professional. See the U.S. Department of Labor's overtime guidance.

Choose the work you need done—not just the lowest price

A human receptionist and an AI receptionist solve overlapping but different problems. A person offers flexible service and can take on a broad range of office responsibilities. AI can provide a lower-cost way to cover defined phone tasks, extend availability and capture information for follow-up. A hybrid approach can combine those strengths when the handoff is designed properly.

Start with your actual call records, define the tasks that can be automated safely and compare the complete cost of each option. If the AI route makes sense, choose a service that will configure and test the workflow with you—not just sell access to another platform.
